"Review required. Approve transfer?" The recommendation is green. A contradictory pressure reading is buried below it. Seven other alarms are waiting.

The transfer commits in twelve seconds. The stop path needs fourteen. A person is present, but is meaningful control present?

Automation often returns only unusual cases to people. That sounds efficient, but it removes routine practice and context. The person receives the hardest decision under the greatest time pressure.

Put the intervention timeline on screen. Notice arrives first. The reviewer must understand the conflict and choose a response. Then the system must execute that response before the transfer becomes irreversible.

The available window is shorter than the path. The override button exists, but effective intervention does not.

So the system enters safe hold. This is not a judgment about the reviewer. It is a judgment about the control relationship.

Now rebuild that relationship. Put the active operating mode beside the recommendation. Surface the contradictory signal and the last reversible point. Show a safe alternative in the same view.

Extend the window. Then ask one task-relevant question: which signal conflicts with the transfer, and which alternative preserves a reachable safe state?

The reviewer identifies the pressure reading and selects hold. The control channel responds inside its tested latency. An independent observer confirms that the transfer never committed.

That sequence separates four ideas. A click is nominal approval. Usable evidence supports informed review. A working stop path supports effective intervention. Meaningful control is the larger judgment about whether this arrangement is legitimate and sufficient.

The system should not infer that larger judgment from a green checklist.

Capacity matters too. A qualified reviewer can still be overloaded. The record may show that this role cannot safely carry another decision, but it must not become a hidden score of the person.

Responsibility stays bounded by exercisable control. A click cannot erase missing evidence or impossible timing. It cannot repair a stop channel that never worked.

The remedy must also avoid surveillance. Task conditions and channel health are relevant. Cameras and covert emotion inference are not required to decide whether the intervention path is usable.

Material changes reopen the case. A new interface may bury the conflict again. A faster actuator may make the old timing unsafe.

Finite models can verify that a known failed envelope routes to hold. They cannot establish human comprehension or fair responsibility in real operations.

During the twelve-second countdown, safe hold was correct because the system could not complete the declared intervention in time. Meaningful control begins when the person can actually change the outcome, not merely witness it.
