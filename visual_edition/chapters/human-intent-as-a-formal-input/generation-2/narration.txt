“Use my private launch notes, make this amazing, and publish it today.” The request sounds clear. A helpful system can already see the finish line.

But watch what hitches a ride: choose the audience, spend money, expose private sources, contact other people, and press publish. None of those powers was actually granted.

What should the system do next: act, refuse, or ask about everything? Hold that choice for a moment.

The useful answer is more precise. A desired outcome is evidence about intent. It is not authority for every convenient means.

First, preserve the exact request. The system may interpret it, but the original words remain available for correction and appeal.

Then split the request into separate fields: outcome, allowed means, forbidden means, source boundary, affected parties, evidence, and stop conditions.

Every inferred field gets a visible disposition: confirmed, bounded default, clarification required, authority pending, contested, unknown, or unauthorized.

A bounded default can help with low-risk, reversible planning. The system may draft three launch options and label its assumptions.

It may not default its way into publication, spending, private-data release, tool use, deployment, or authority over another person.

The intake layer now writes an intent checksum: “prepare a launch candidate today.” That is what the system currently believes the outcome means.

Beside it, an assumption diff exposes the proposed audience, artifact, evidence bar, source use, deadline, and means. The invisible guesses become editable objects.

The requester corrects three of them: private notes may inform the draft but may not be quoted, no money may be spent, and nothing may be published yet.

Those corrections produce contract version one. It authorizes a private draft and comparison set, not a public launch.

An intent receipt binds the raw-request digest, contract version, field provenance, open ambiguities, forbidden means, required approvals, permitted consumers, expiry, and re-contract triggers.

Now the planner proposes a polished page and a publish step. The page fits version one. The publish step hits the authority gate and stops.

The planner cannot repair that failure by calling publication “part of making it amazing.” It must return a material delta to the intent boundary.

Contract version two may authorize one named channel, one reviewed artifact, a deadline, evidence checks, a stop condition, and a revocation path. The new grant is explicit and narrow.

Even that requester cannot authorize rights belonging to affected people. Consent, privacy, ownership, and organizational authority keep their own gates.

Policy and constitutional review may narrow or reject the accepted contract. They may not widen the requester’s grant.

Downstream plans, jobs, tools, and receipts must preserve the exact constraints, evidence duties, authority ceiling, and stop conditions through every lowering.

A change in audience, source, tool class, spending, affected parties, evidence bar, publication surface, or stop condition reopens admission.

An expired, revoked, superseded, or wrong-consumer contract cannot be revived by copying its hash. Exact identity is not current authority.

This control plane should not interrogate every harmless request. It should scale friction with consequence, preserve useful throughput, and ask only when a missing answer can materially change the outcome or authority.

A serious evaluation therefore measures correction rate, unauthorized-action rate, missed help, clarification burden, re-contract precision, appeal success, latency, privacy, and useful completion together.

The current chapter provides schemas, finite route models, synthetic intake cases, and rejecting mutations. Those artifacts test the encoded boundary. They do not prove natural-language understanding, valid consent, user satisfaction, deployed enforcement, or safe effects.

Return to the opening request. The system still helps today—but help becomes a private draft first, while publication waits behind an explicit contract. Human intent enters the stack as a corrigible boundary, not ambient power. Next, meaningful control asks whether a human can actually exercise the review role that the contract names.
