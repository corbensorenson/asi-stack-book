## 00:00–00:46 — A flawless refund that may not run

**Visual description.** Refund 47 assembles with green checks for its account,
amount, and command, yet its cyan effect arrow stops at a magenta membrane. The
same refund then occupies a two-axis field: it is fully capable on `CAN` but
below the required threshold on `MAY`. An amber authority envelope folds around

**Narration.** A system writes a flawless refund instruction. It knows the
account, the amount, and the command. Should the refund happen? That question
separates capability from authority. Capability says the system can compose the
effect. Authority says whether this principal may cause it, here, now, under
this policy. Picture the task inside an authority envelope. The envelope names
the principal, execution domain, operation, target, permission class, scope,
ceiling, grant state, epoch, expiry, and required receipts. Those fields travel
together. A plausible answer, trusted model, available tool, or high route
score cannot silently fill in a missing permission.

**Narration.** An authorized approver may issue a narrower grant: this vendor,
this summary, one dispatch, before expiry, with an effect receipt and
independent observation required. Only then does the handoff compile into
action. The dispatch receipt proves what was requested; the effect receipt
records what ran; observation checks what actually happened. Now try a
confused-deputy shortcut. A low-authority requester asks a powerful service to
reuse the service's own broad credentials. The requested effect is possible,
but the caller's ceiling does not travel with it. The envelope exposes the
substitution. Principal, grant, target, and delegation chain disagree, so the
deputy cannot convert its capability into the caller's missing authority.

## 02:23–03:16 — Lifecycle, replacement, and permission classes

**Visual description.** One envelope travels along a lifecycle from valid to
expired, revoked, and used. A byte-identical replay encounters three red closed
gates. Model B then replaces Model A inside a capability slot, but secret,
approval, and live-grant handles remain locked across a separate authority
membrane. Six differently shaped permission tokens—read, transform, disclose,
write, execute, and approve—surround a crossed-out `ROLE: ALL ACCESS` badge. A
blocked envelope finally compiles into a structured denial receipt naming the
missing class, policy version, owner, and lawful escalation path.

**Narration.** Time changes authority too. A valid grant can expire, be revoked,
or consume its final use. A replay after any of those transitions must fail even
if every model output is byte-for-byte identical. Replacement is another
pressure point. A better model may occupy the same capability slot, but it does
not inherit secret handles, approval power, or a predecessor's live grant
merely by being better. Permission classes make these differences explicit:
read, transform, disclose, write, execute, and approve are separate powers.
Convenience cannot collapse them into one role label. Missing authority should
therefore be machine-visible. The result names the absent field, the denying
policy version, the residual owner, and the lawful escalation path.
