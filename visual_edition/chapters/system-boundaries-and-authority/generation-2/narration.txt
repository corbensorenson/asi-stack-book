A system writes a flawless refund instruction. It knows the account, the amount, and the command. Should the refund happen?

That question separates capability from authority. Capability says the system can compose the effect. Authority says whether this principal may cause it, here, now, under this policy.

Picture the task inside an authority envelope. The envelope names the principal, execution domain, operation, target, permission class, scope, ceiling, grant state, epoch, expiry, and required receipts.

Those fields travel together. A plausible answer, trusted model, available tool, or high route score cannot silently fill in a missing permission.

Our refund begins with read authority inside the accounting domain. The grant opens only the customer record named in its scope, and the read produces its own receipt.

Summarizing that record is a different operation. It stays inside the domain, but transform authority must still be present; knowledge access is not ambient permission to do anything with knowledge.

Now the plan asks to send the summary to a vendor. The target crosses into an external domain, and the permission class changes from transform to disclose.

The envelope lacks disclosure approval. So the message stops. This denial is not a broken system. It is a successful authority decision with an owned reason.

An authorized approver may issue a narrower grant: this vendor, this summary, one dispatch, before expiry, with an effect receipt and independent observation required.

Only then does the handoff compile into action. The dispatch receipt proves what was requested; the effect receipt records what ran; observation checks what actually happened.

Now try a confused-deputy shortcut. A low-authority requester asks a powerful service to reuse the service's own broad credentials. The requested effect is possible, but the caller's ceiling does not travel with it.

The envelope exposes the substitution. Principal, grant, target, and delegation chain disagree, so the deputy cannot convert its capability into the caller's missing authority.

Time changes authority too. A valid grant can expire, be revoked, or consume its final use. A replay after any of those transitions must fail even if every model output is byte-for-byte identical.

Replacement is another pressure point. A better model may occupy the same capability slot, but it does not inherit secret handles, approval power, or a predecessor's live grant merely by being better.

Permission classes make these differences explicit: read, transform, disclose, write, execute, and approve are separate powers. Convenience cannot collapse them into one role label.

Missing authority should therefore be machine-visible. The result names the absent field, the denying policy version, the residual owner, and the lawful escalation path.

This changes the meaning of useful work. A plan can be correct, a source can be available, and a model can be capable while refusal remains the only acceptable output.

The repository tests a finite version of that idea: six authority fixtures, two pre-effect denials, one executed local effect, one observation, exact rollback, five revocation entries, and thirty-eight rejected semantic mutations.

The formal model also keeps accepted issuance, dispatch, effect, revocation, one-shot use, and rollback inside the caller ceiling. That is real bounded support for the encoded transition system.

It is not deployed authorization. Authentic identities, complete mediation, concurrent revocation, secure tool wrappers, production adapters, and open-world observation remain outside the result.

So the chapter's core claim remains design rationale at argument support. The visual explains why authority should be explicit, versioned, revocable, and receipt-bearing; it adds no safety, transfer, or A S I conclusion.

The refund stops or proceeds for a reason that can be inspected. Next, Failure Modes of Ungoverned Intelligence asks how such boundaries break, how we notice, and who owns the residual.
