Four routes can read the same invoice. A parser costs one unit. A specialist costs four. A frontier model costs nine. A human review costs more. Which route is efficient? Choose from the fares you can see.

Price alone cannot answer. The invoice must preserve the total and the vendor. It must also obey the privacy rule. Efficiency begins with an acceptance test, not a model menu.

Send a familiar invoice to the one-unit parser. It extracts every field and passes the test. For this case, the cheap route looks excellent.
Now damage one digit in the tax line. The parser still returns an answer, but the total is wrong. Repair costs eight units. Review costs three more.
The one-unit route has become a twelve-unit route. The verified nine-unit route is now cheaper, even before the downstream error reaches accounting.

That reversal is the Efficient ASI hypothesis in miniature. The efficient route is the least costly authorized route that satisfies the whole contract. It is not the route with the smallest visible meter.
A router can learn this boundary. Familiar templates go to the parser. Uncertain scans escalate to the specialist. High-consequence cases may go directly to a stronger model or a person.
The failed attempt stays on the bill. Otherwise a routing policy can appear efficient by hiding retries and repairs outside its own measurement window.

Reuse can compound the gain. Repeated verified work may become a parser or a cached procedure. But the artifact keeps its assumptions. When the invoice format changes, the route expires until it is checked again.

Now change the case. Suppose review becomes nearly free, but privacy risk rises sharply. Would the cheapest compute route still win? Hold both changes in view before you choose.
No single answer follows. The complete bill changes the decision because costs caused at one layer surface in another. Model and context spending enter alongside verification and repair. Human review, regression upkeep, and rollback or residual burden finish the same seven-class total.

A fair experiment would freeze a natural workload and compare several routing policies under matched access. It would count useful accepted work and every material burden created along the way.
Lean proves a narrower result here. Over an authored finite list, the selector returns an eligible route with no greater declared seven-class total than any eligible candidate. It does not prove that the list is complete, the declared costs are true, or any measured efficiency or ASI result.
The practical test is simple: a saving is real only if it remains after quality, authority, verification, fallback, and repair have all been paid.
