Northline is a corridor shared by two jurisdictions. It uses one flood-response model and one river. A routing decision in one district can move risk across the border.

The model passes its technical checks. One government authorizes emergency routing. The other requires public review before cross-border diversion.

Who has authority to let the system act?

Technical conformance cannot answer. A standard can show that a control behaved as specified. It cannot create a public mandate.

Build an authority map. Each jurisdiction keeps its legal role. River communities receive standing because they bear the effect. The review forum must remain challengeable.

The first assessor is paid by the model provider and sees only a curated dashboard. The report may still contain useful evidence, but its independence and access are limited.

The institution pauses authorization until protected logs become available to another reviewer.

Then a second gap appears. A border settlement and seasonal workers were absent from participation. Counting the people who attended does not repair the missing standing.

Add a representative route and language support. Give the settlement access to the evidence and a way to appeal.

Now run the tabletop. The model keeps the compute grid online, but it diverts cooling water toward measured districts. The unmeasured settlement loses capacity.

The technical score remains green. The distributional effect does not.

The routing plan stops. Both authorities receive the trace. The dispute process opens, and the remedy clock begins.

This separation matters. Technical success is one claim. Legal force is another. Public legitimacy asks whether the people bearing risk can contest the decision and reach a remedy.

One claim cannot average away failure in another.

Emergency authority also needs an expiry. A crisis may justify a faster route, but it cannot create permanent power by default.

Withdrawal must leave custody behind. If a jurisdiction exits the agreement, incidents already in flight still need evidence and an accountable remedy owner.

The same is true for a successor system. Copying the old charter does not transfer legitimacy when the population or verifier has changed.

Finite tabletop records can test conflict routing and missing-standing gates. They cannot establish public trust or geopolitical stability. They do not prove genuine consent.

Northline's model is technically bounded. The institution becomes governable only when affected communities can see the basis of action and challenge the authority carrying risk across the river.
