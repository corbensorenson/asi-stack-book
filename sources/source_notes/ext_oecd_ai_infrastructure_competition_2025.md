## Thesis

The report maps the compute supply chain across chip design, fabrication,
specialized equipment and materials, servers, data centers, energy, networks,
cloud providers, and model developers. It identifies concentration, barriers
to entry, vertical integration, partnerships, cross-holdings, scarcity, and
state intervention as recurring features.

## Failure Modes

- Market shares, bottlenecks, and theories of harm are time- and
  jurisdiction-dependent.
- Concentration is not itself proof of unlawful conduct, and fragmentation is
  not automatically resilient or efficient.
- A technical interoperability interface does not by itself create effective
  competition when data, capital, power, talent, or contracts remain locked.

## Mechanisms

Control can accumulate through scarce fabrication capacity, accelerator
supply, cloud access, energy interconnection, data, contractual tying,
partnerships, and vertical integration. These mechanisms can make nominal
model portability economically ineffective.

## Evidence

The report provides an institutionally grounded map of current infrastructure
layers and competition concerns. Its measurements and legal implications
remain market-, time-, and jurisdiction-specific.

## Claims To Add Or Update

- Deployment readiness must include concentration, substitutability, exit,
  and bottleneck metrics, not only aggregate compute capacity.
- Architectural modularity creates practical agency only when organizations
  can exercise migration rights across data, models, and infrastructure.

## Open Questions

- Which portability tests best predict actual switching rather than paper
  compatibility?
- When does public or shared compute increase resilience without creating a
  new centralized dependency?
