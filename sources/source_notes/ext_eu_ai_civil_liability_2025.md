## Thesis

The study analyzes civil-liability allocation for AI and argues for a
harmonized approach centered on high-risk systems and a responsible operator.
It gives the book a concrete counterweight to vague “a human remains
accountable” language: responsibility needs an identifiable entity, evidence
access, causal and burden rules, remedy, and an institutional path that works
after harm.

## Failure Modes

- The recommendation is jurisdiction- and policy-specific.
- Technical provenance does not decide causation, fault, standing, damages, or
  legal responsibility.
- Liability can improve incentives or create perverse avoidance; empirical
  effects must be measured rather than assumed.

## Mechanisms

Liability operates through assignment of responsible operators, definitions
of high-risk activity, access to evidence, burdens of proof, causation rules,
insurance, damages, and procedural routes to remedy.

## Evidence

The study is evidence for a concrete European policy design space and its
tradeoffs. It is not evidence that the recommendations are enacted,
universally applicable, or empirically optimal.

## Claims To Add Or Update

- Human oversight is not accountability unless a harmed party can identify a
  responsible entity, obtain relevant evidence, and pursue a usable remedy.
- Technical logs should preserve facts needed by institutions while avoiding
  automated conclusions about legal fault.

## Open Questions

- How should burdens of proof change when only providers can inspect the
  decisive system evidence?
- Which insurance and compensation structures improve incentives without
  excluding small or public-interest deployers?
