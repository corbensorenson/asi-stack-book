## What entertainment means here

An ASI Stack video is entertaining when the viewer wants to see the next
meaningful state change. Interest should come from a concrete problem,
prediction, progressive reveal, surprising consequence, or elegant visual
compression. Motion, jokes, music, or novelty that does not support the
teaching promise is extraneous material, not engagement.

Use this loop:

1. **Expectation:** show a familiar object or plausible shortcut.
2. **Tension:** reveal a mismatch, hidden constraint, or pending consequence.
3. **Prediction:** ask what will happen and allow a real thinking pause.
4. **Mechanism:** change the relevant objects in causal order.
5. **Payoff:** make the result visible, then name the general idea.
6. **Transfer:** alter one condition and let the viewer predict again.

The loop creates narrative energy without turning a technical claim into a
dramatic overclaim.

## Cognitive-load rules

Working memory is limited. Use the visual and auditory channels to carry
complementary information, not duplicate prose.

- **Coherence:** remove unrelated words, pictures, sounds, and movements.
- **Signaling:** highlight the active object, relation, or stage with a short
  label, arrow, contrast change, or focus isolation.
- **Segmenting:** divide the explanation into semantic beats and allow the
  viewer to complete one state change before the next begins.
- **Spatial contiguity:** place a label beside the object or relation it names.
- **Temporal contiguity:** show the corresponding change while it is being
  described, not a paragraph before or after.
- **Modality:** use narration for causal explanation and visuals for objects,
  relations, quantities, sequence, and state.
- **Redundancy control:** do not place full narrated sentences on screen.
- **Pretraining:** briefly establish unfamiliar object names or symbols before
  running a dense mechanism.

These principles do not imply an empty screen. The goal is high semantic
density with low irrelevant density.

## Curiosity and story

Open with value, not administration. Within 15 seconds, the viewer should see
the title's promise embodied as a decision, puzzle, failure, or change. A good
hook is honest: it introduces the same question the video will answer.

Prefer chapter-specific story forms:

- follow one token through a stack until an authority gate stops it;
- race a fast unsafe route against a slower governed route;
- remove a backup and expose why weight rollback is incomplete;
- let two plausible memories compete, then reveal their authority domains;
- construct a mechanism piece by piece, then break one assumption;
- begin with a confident prediction and visually falsify it; or
- compare two architectures on one job rather than reciting a taxonomy.

Use callbacks. Reuse the opening object in the worked trace, counterexample,
and final payoff so the viewer experiences resolution rather than a sequence
of unrelated slides.

## Active-viewing prompts

Prompts should make the viewer use the model being taught. In a 3–6 minute
video, one or two strong prediction prompts are usually better than frequent
decorative questions.

For each prompt:

1. Put enough evidence on screen to answer it.
2. Ask one specific question.
3. Hold for roughly 1–3 seconds, scaled to difficulty.
4. Animate the consequence.
5. Explain why, using the same objects.

Do not ask a question and answer it in the same breath. Do not use trivia,
generic rhetorical questions, or prompts whose answer never changes the
visual state.

## Writing and performance

Write spoken language, not paper prose. Use one idea per sentence, active
voice, concrete verbs, contractions where natural, and causal transitions.
Keep the tone direct and curious. Let sentence length and emphasis change with
the action: short at a decision or reveal, slightly longer during a stable
explanation.

The synthesized voice still needs performance direction. Mark emphasis,
pauses, questions, contrasts, and pronunciation before synthesis. Listen for
flat cadence, repeated sentence shapes, and identical scene openings. If the
voice cannot make a sentence sound natural, rewrite the sentence.

## Measurement and inference limits

Use YouTube audience retention after publication to find moments worth
reviewing. A first-30-second loss can implicate a promise mismatch or slow
opening. Dips can indicate boredom, confusion, or a skippable section. Spikes
can indicate interest or the need to rewatch something unclear. None of these
metrics proves learning.

Record a hypothesis before revision, then compare the replacement with the
prior generation. Keep the chapter, duration range, audience, and packaging
differences in view. Pair retention with the pedagogical review: can a viewer
predict the worked trace, explain the mechanism, and state the boundary?

Research boundaries matter:

- Guo, Kim, and Rubin studied viewing behavior across 6.9 million MOOC video
  sessions. Their results are observational engagement evidence, not a causal
  proof of learning or a universal six-minute law.
- Brame's review organizes practice around cognitive load, engagement, and
  active learning; it is a synthesis, not a guarantee for every audience.
- A 2022 physics-video experiment found its strongest learning result when
  enhanced visuals and embedded questions were combined. Preserve the domain
  and study boundary rather than universalizing the effect.
